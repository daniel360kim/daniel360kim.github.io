## Background

3DGS is a method for novel view synthesis: generating new images of a scene from viewpoints that weren't in the original dataset. It represents a scene as a bunch of 3D Gaussians and renders them efficiently at high quality using a technique called Gaussian splatting.

I'm interested in it mainly because it could give reinforcement learning agents a cheap way to train inside realistic 3D environments, which could help with generalizing to the real world.

## High-level overview

I like to get a high-level picture of a method before diving into details, so I know where each step fits while I'm implementing it.

### Scene representation
The scene is represented by millions of 3D Gaussians, each with a position, covariance, color, and opacity. 

For any viewpoint, we use the camera's pose to project these Gaussians to a 2D image, resulting in a bunch of overlaid 2D Gaussians.

We then use a technique called alpha compositing to add up the Gaussians at every pixel to create the image.

## The math

A 3D Gaussian can be written as:
$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right)$$
where:
- $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is a point in 3D space
- $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the mean (center) of the Gaussian
- $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ is the covariance matrix, which sets the shape and orientation of the Gaussian

Note this isn't normalized to integrate to 1 — that's fine, since the opacity term below absorbs whatever scaling is missing.

A scene is millions of these Gaussians. Each Gaussian $i$ carries:
- $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$: its mean
- $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i$: its covariance
- $c_i$: its color (RGB for now)
- $o_i \in [0, 1]$: its opacity

## Projection and rendering

3DGS projects the mean and covariance of each Gaussian onto the image plane using the standard pinhole camera model. The mean projects the obvious way; the covariance takes a little more work.

### The pinhole camera model, briefly

The pinhole camera model describes how a camera maps a 3D scene onto a 2D image: light rays pass through a single point (the pinhole) and land on the image plane. The projection is:
$$\tilde{\mathbf{p}} = \mathbf{K} [\mathbf{R} \mid \mathbf{t}] \mathbf{X}$$
where:
- $\tilde{\mathbf{p}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the homogeneous coordinates of the projected point
- $\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ is the camera intrinsic matrix (focal length, principal point)
- $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ is the camera's rotation
- $\mathbf{t} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the camera's translation
- $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the 3D point in the scene

This splits into two steps:
1. **World to camera**: rotate and translate the point into camera coordinates:
$$\mathbf{X}_c = \mathbf{R} \mathbf{X} + \mathbf{t}$$
2. **Camera to image**: project onto the 2D image plane with the intrinsic matrix:
$$\tilde{\mathbf{p}} = \mathbf{K} \mathbf{X}_c$$

where $\mathbf{K} = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, with $f_x, f_y$ the focal lengths in pixels and $(c_x, c_y)$ the principal point.

Dividing the homogeneous coordinates by the third component gives pixel coordinates:
$$\mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \tilde{p}_x / \tilde{p}_z \\ \tilde{p}_y / \tilde{p}_z \end{bmatrix}$$

### Projecting the Gaussian mean

Using this, the mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$ of each Gaussian projects straight through the pinhole model:
$$\mathbf{p}_i = \mathbf{K} [\mathbf{R} \mid \mathbf{t}] \boldsymbol{\mu}_i$$
That gives the 2D coordinates of the Gaussian's center in the image.

### Projecting the Gaussian covariance

Projecting the covariance is more involved. The key rule: if $y = Ax + b$ is a linear map and $x$ has covariance $\Sigma_x$, then $y$ has covariance $\Sigma_y = A \Sigma_x A^\top$ — the translation $b$ drops out entirely, since shifting a distribution doesn't change how it's spread around its mean. ([Section 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.04079) of the 3DGS paper spells this out; the whole trick is borrowed from Zwicker et al.'s EWA splatting.)

The world-to-camera step is linear, so this rule applies directly:
$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i,cam} = \mathbf{R} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i \mathbf{R}^\top$$

But the camera-to-image step is *not* linear:
$$\pi(x, y, z) = \left( \frac{f_x x}{z} + c_x, \frac{f_y y}{z} + c_y \right)$$

Since each Gaussian is concentrated tightly around its mean, we can approximate $\pi$ near $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$ with a first-order Taylor expansion:
$$\pi(\mathbf{x}) \approx \pi(\boldsymbol{\mu}_i) + J_\pi(\boldsymbol{\mu}_i)(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_i)$$

where $J_\pi(\boldsymbol{\mu}_i)$ is the Jacobian of the projection evaluated at the mean:
$$J_\pi(\boldsymbol{\mu}_i) = \begin{bmatrix}
\frac{f_x}{\mu_{i,z}} & 0 & -\frac{f_x \mu_{i,x}}{\mu_{i,z}^2} \\
0 & \frac{f_y}{\mu_{i,z}} & -\frac{f_y \mu_{i,y}}{\mu_{i,z}^2}
\end{bmatrix}$$

With the projection now linearized, the covariance rule applies a second time:
$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i,img} = J_\pi(\boldsymbol{\mu_i}) \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i,cam} J_\pi(\boldsymbol{\mu_i})^\top$$

Putting it together, each Gaussian's projected mean and covariance in image coordinates are:
- Mean: $\mathbf{p}_i = \mathbf{K} [\mathbf{R} \mid \mathbf{t}] \boldsymbol{\mu}_i$
- Covariance: $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i,img} = J_\pi(\boldsymbol{\mu_i}) \mathbf{R} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i \mathbf{R}^\top J_\pi(\boldsymbol{\mu_i})^\top$

That's the whole projection step — every Gaussian in the scene turns into a 2D ellipse on the image, positioned at $\mathbf{p}_i$ and shaped by $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i,img}$.

## Alpha compositing

Now that we have a bunch of Gaussians projected onto the image plane, we need to combine them into the final image. This is alpha compositing.

For an arbitrary pixel $p$, we take the $n$ Gaussians that actually contribute to it and sort them by depth, so Gaussian 1 is closest to the camera and Gaussian $n$ is farthest. Each contributes:
* $\alpha_i = o_i \cdot \text{G}_{2D}(\mathbf{p}; \mathbf{p}_i, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i,img})$ — opacity times the Gaussian's value at that pixel
* $c_i \in [0, 1]^3$ — the Gaussian's color

### Transmittance

$T_i$ is the transmittance: the fraction of light that survives all $i-1$ splats in front of it. It's computed recursively:
$$T_1 = 1, \quad T_i = T_{i-1} (1 - \alpha_{i-1}) = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j)$$

That's just the product of the transparency $(1 - \alpha)$ of every splat in front of it.

The final pixel color is a weighted sum of the splat colors, weighted by transmittance and opacity:
$$C(p) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} T_i \alpha_i c_i = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j) \right) \alpha_i c_i$$

Do this over every pixel and you get the rendered image from that viewpoint.

## Learning a 3DGS model

Once we can render a scene from any viewpoint, we can learn the Gaussian parameters — means, covariances, colors, opacities — from a set of training images. The training loop is simple:
1. Initialize the Gaussians' parameters randomly or with a heuristic.
2. For each training image, render the scene from the corresponding viewpoint using the current parameters.
3. Compute the loss between the rendered image and the ground-truth image.
4. Backpropagate and update the parameters with gradient descent.

## Recap

I always find it helpful to recap all the steps so I can see how they fit together:
1. Represent the scene as a collection of 3D Gaussians, each with a mean, covariance, color, and opacity.
2. For each viewpoint, project the means and covariances onto the image plane via the pinhole model and the projection Jacobian.
3. For each pixel, gather the contributions of every Gaussian that lands on it and combine them with alpha compositing.
4. Repeat step 2 and 3 for any viewpoint to render the scene from there.
5. Learn the Gaussians' parameters from training images by rendering, computing the loss, and updating with gradient descent.

### Representing a Gaussian, the numerically-safe way

The math says each Gaussian carries a covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, but you can't just make $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ a learnable $3\times3$ matrix. A covariance has to stay symmetric positive-definite, and a raw gradient step will happily walk it somewhere that isn't — at which point $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}$ blows up and the Gaussian is meaningless.

The trick from the paper is to store the *factors* instead and rebuild $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ so it's valid by construction:
$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = R S S^\top R^\top$$
where $S = \text{diag}(s_x, s_y, s_z)$ is a positive scale and $R$ is a rotation built from a (normalized) quaternion. Anything you feed in comes back out as a legitimate covariance, because $R S S^\top R^\top$ is positive-definite for *any* $R$ and any nonzero $S$.

```python
def quat_to_R(q):
    q = F.normalize(q, dim=-1)                    # keep it a unit quaternion
    w, x, y, z = q.unbind(-1)
    return torch.stack([
        1 - 2*(y**2 + z**2), 2*(x*y - w*z),     2*(x*z + w*y),
        2*(x*y + w*z),       1 - 2*(x**2 + z**2), 2*(y*z - w*x),
        2*(x*z - w*y),       2*(y*z + w*x),     1 - 2*(x**2 + y**2),
    ]).reshape(3, 3)

def build_covariance(scale, quat):
    RS = quat_to_R(quat) @ torch.diag(scale)
    return RS @ RS.T                              # Σ = (RS)(RS)ᵀ = R S Sᵀ Rᵀ
```

Quick sanity check that pins down the whole idea: feed in the identity quaternion $(1,0,0,0)$, so $R = I$, and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ should collapse to $\text{diag}(s_x^2, s_y^2, s_z^2)$ — an axis-aligned ellipsoid, no rotation. That's an exact number you can verify by hand, which is the kind of check I lean on the whole way through.

### World to camera

This is the exact, linear part of the projection. The mean transforms as an ordinary rigid motion, and because the map is linear, the covariance rule $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{cam} = R\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,R^\top$ from the math section applies *exactly* — no approximation yet.

```python
def world_to_cam_point(x, R_cw, t_cw):
    return R_cw @ x + t_cw

def world_to_cam_covariance(Sigma, R_cw):
    return R_cw @ Sigma @ R_cw.T                  # Σ_cam = R Σ Rᵀ
```

The translation $t_{cw}$ shows up for the point but not the covariance — exactly the "shifting a distribution doesn't change its spread" point from earlier.

### Projecting the mean and the Jacobian

The mean projects straight through the pinhole map $\pi(x,y,z) = (f_x x/z + c_x,\ f_y y/z + c_y)$. The covariance can't, because of that $1/z$ — the map is nonlinear. So we linearize it at the mean with the Jacobian:
$$J = \begin{bmatrix} f_x/z & 0 & -f_x x/z^2 \\ 0 & f_y/z & -f_y y/z^2 \end{bmatrix}$$

```python
def project_point(x_cam, fx, fy, cx, cy):
    x, y, z = x_cam
    return torch.stack([fx * x / z + cx, fy * y / z + cy])

def perspective_jacobian(x_cam, fx, fy):
    x, y, z = x_cam
    return torch.tensor([
        [fx / z, 0.,     -fx * x / z**2],
        [0.,     fy / z, -fy * y / z**2],
    ])
```

You don't have to trust the hand-derived Jacobian — you can check it numerically. Nudge the input by a tiny $\epsilon$, measure how much $\pi$ actually moved, and compare against $J\epsilon$. That finite-difference check is worth keeping as a habit any time you write a Jacobian by hand:

```python
eps = 1e-4
for i in range(3):
    dx = torch.zeros(3); dx[i] = eps
    numeric = (project_point(x + dx, fx, fy, 0, 0)
               - project_point(x - dx, fx, fy, 0, 0)) / (2 * eps)
    assert torch.allclose(J[:, i], numeric, atol=1e-2)
```

### EWA splatting: the 2D covariance

With $J$ in hand, apply the covariance rule one more time — now with the linear *approximation* $J$ standing in for the exact map — to land the ellipse on the image plane:
$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D} = J\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{cam}\,J^\top$$

```python
def project_covariance_to_2d(Sigma_cam, x_cam, fx, fy):
    J = perspective_jacobian(x_cam, fx, fy)
    Sigma2D = J @ Sigma_cam @ J.T
    return Sigma2D + 0.15 * torch.eye(2)          # low-pass dilation, see below
```

That last term is the one bit of the code the math above doesn't predict. A Gaussian that projects to smaller than a pixel would fall between the pixel-center samples and vanish, flickering as the camera moves. Adding a small constant to the diagonal — the EWA "low-pass filter" — guarantees every splat is at least about a pixel wide, so it always gets sampled. It's a rendering fudge, not part of the ideal math, but you need it.

### Evaluating a splat and compositing

Two pieces left, and both are direct transcriptions. A projected Gaussian's value at a pixel is just the quadratic form:
$$G_{2D}(p) = \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(p - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{2D})^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}^{-1}(p - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{2D})\right)$$

```python
def eval_gaussian_2d(pixel, mean2d, Sigma2D_inv):
    diff = pixel - mean2d
    return torch.exp(-0.5 * diff @ Sigma2D_inv @ diff)
```

(A splat is worth exactly $1.0$ at its own center — the quadratic form is zero there — which is a handy invariant when a render looks wrong.) Then alpha compositing front-to-back, which is the $C(p) = \sum_i T_i \alpha_i c_i$ from the math with the transmittance written as an *exclusive* cumulative product:

```python
def composite(colors, alphas):                    # sorted front-to-back
    T = torch.cumprod(torch.cat([torch.ones(1), 1 - alphas[:-1]]), dim=0)
    weights = alphas * T                          # Tᵢ = ∏_{j<i}(1 - αⱼ)
    return (weights.unsqueeze(-1) * colors).sum(dim=0), weights
```

The `torch.cat([ones, 1 - alphas[:-1]])` is what makes the product *exclusive*: splat $i$'s weight sees everything in front of it but not itself, which is exactly $T_i = \prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_j)$.

### Scaling up and training

Going from one Gaussian to a scene is mostly a batching exercise — every $(3,3)$ becomes $(N,3,3)$, and the per-pixel Gaussian evaluation becomes one `einsum` over all pixels and all splats at once:

```python
diff = pix[:, None] - mean_2d[None]               # (P, N, 2)
quad = torch.einsum("pni,nij,pnj->pn", diff, Sigma2D_inv, diff)
alpha = (opacity * torch.exp(-0.5 * quad)).clamp(0, 0.999)
```

The `clamp(0, 0.999)` keeps a fully-opaque splat from zeroing out the transmittance of everything behind it (which would kill their gradients), and depth gets a `clamp(min=1e-3)` so a Gaussian drifting toward the image plane doesn't divide by zero. Small guards, but training is unstable without them.

Once the renderer is differentiable end-to-end, learning is the obvious loop: render a view, take MSE against the ground-truth image, backprop into every parameter — means, scales, quaternions, opacities, colors. I built a toy scene of two colored clouds, rendered it from six orbiting cameras as the "training data," then fit a fresh set of 120 Gaussians to those views:

```python
def train_one_step(model, cams, targets, optim, step):
    cam = cams[step % len(cams)]
    loss = F.mse_loss(render(model, cam), targets[step % len(cams)])
    optim.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); optim.step()
    return loss
```
